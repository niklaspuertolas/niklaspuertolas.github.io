📝 TL;DR

Copilot v2 is a chat interface inside the Landbot builder that gets users from “I want an agent that does X” to a working bot — without making them learn the canvas first. It shipped in late January 2026, replacing a v1 MVP that had hit technical limits we couldn’t get past.

The hard part wasn’t the AI. It was how much personality to strip out. Power users hit Copilot constantly, and at that volume, friendly turns into noise. So v2 went the other way: calm, competent, and direct.

📈 46% → 64% — trial users who use Copilot and then publish a bot, from Dec 2025 to Apr 2026 (5,000+ interactions).

My role: led UX/UI and authored the persona & behavior framework — with a design supervisor, one PM, and three engineers.

🎯 Context

Landbot is a conversational agent builder used mostly by go-to-market teams. The product is a visual flow editor where you wire up blocks: ask a question, branch on the answer, send a message, hit an integration. It works, and it’s flexible. But it asks you to think in flow logic before you’ve thought about your bot.

Our main user persona internally is Michael, a technical GTM operator. For Michael, the canvas is a manageable amount of friction. For non-technical users, it’s often the place where the project quietly dies.

AI shifted what users expect on top of that. People now show up to a builder expecting they can describe what they want and have the tool meet them somewhere in the middle. A conversational agent builder that doesn’t itself feel conversational starts to feel out of step.

We started designing Copilot in Q4 2025, a chat interface that lives inside the builder and helps users get to a working bot faster. My scope as Product Designer was the end-to-end UX and UI, plus the persona and behaviour model. I worked alongside a design supervisor, one PM, and three engineers.

🧩 The problem

Users wanted a working agent. The canvas asked them to figure out how to build one first.

Landbot’s flow builder is powerful, but it puts a lot on the first-time user. Take the goal most of them come in with: “capture and qualify leads from my website.” That single sentence becomes a sequence of decisions on the canvas. Ask for a name. Validate the email. Branch based on which service they’re interested in. Route the qualified ones to a team. Send a confirmation. Each of those is a block, with options, and the canvas can’t help you make any of those calls because it doesn’t know what you’re trying to build.

The data lined up with what we suspected. Of users who finished onboarding, the ones who never built anything activated at around 3%. The ones who at least started building manually got to 12%. The bottleneck wasn’t capability. It was getting started.

The opportunity was clear: shorten the distance between “I want an agent that captures and qualifies leads from my pricing page” and a working canvas, without taking the canvas away from anyone who actually wants to use it.

🔍 Research and framing

What we knew before redesigning

The first version of Copilot was built using Landbot itself. It worked as a proof of concept, but we hit technical limits we couldn’t engineer around, and the team made the call to rebuild it from scratch on different infrastructure. That gave us an opening: v2 didn’t have to be just a re-platforming exercise. We could use the rebuild to fix what wasn’t working in the product itself.

I didn’t have time for a long discovery cycle, so I pulled from four sources — Amplitude data, support tickets, internal stakeholders, and competitor research. They all pointed the same way: users arrive with intent and leave without a bot.

What each source told me

Amplitude data on activation and stickiness. The numbers were ugly in the way you’d expect. A meaningful chunk of users landed in the builder and either dropped off without producing anything or used it once and never came back. The shape of the problem pointed at a specific gap: users were arriving with intent and leaving without a bot.

Support tickets. Tickets clustered around a single category: “how do I do X.” How do I build this specific flow. How do I set up this custom integration. How do I wire up a particular action. People weren’t questioning whether Landbot could do these things. They were stuck on translating what they wanted into the canvas.

Conversations with internal stakeholders. I talked to colleagues across sales and customer-facing teams. They described the same pattern in different language. Users come in with strong intent, the product can do the thing, and the gap in the middle is where adoption falls apart. The customer-facing folks in particular flagged how often they end up walking customers through builds that the product, in theory, should make obvious.

Competitor research. I looked at two groups. The AI chat leaders, Lovable, Claude, Gemini, for how they structure responses, handle uncertainty, and pace interactions. And the embedded copilots, n8n, Notion, Gumloop, Zapier, Amplitude’s own, for how a copilot lives inside a product without taking it over. The second group taught me more. The good ones complement the existing tool. The weak ones feel like a chatbot bolted onto a different product.

Three insights that framed v2

1. The friction isn’t capability. It’s translation. Users come in knowing what they want. Landbot can do it. What’s missing is the path between “I want to qualify leads from my pricing page” and a working flow. v2 had to translate first and execute second.

2. Copilot is going to be used a lot. Defaults need to scale. Our most active builders, the “Michaels,” generate 3 to 4 times more events per brand than average. If they use Copilot at any meaningful rate, they’ll hit it hundreds of times a week. That meant the defaults, tone, density, animation, response length, all of it, had to hold up under repeated use. Not just feel good in a demo.

3. Trust is the actual product. Every interaction with Copilot either builds trust or chips away at it. A wrong action you can’t undo. A vague answer when you needed a clear one. Capability that flashes briefly and then fails. The behavior model needed to make trust the constraint, not just one consideration among many.

🎭 Defining Copilot’s persona and behavior

A platform feature with a voice, not a character

A lot of AI assistants lean into personality. They get a name, a backstory, sometimes a face. We went a different direction. Copilot isn’t a separate entity sitting next to Landbot, it’s Landbot speaking through the builder. It says “I can help you add a block,” not “Landbot can help you.” There’s no act, no moods, no character to maintain. It has a job.

How I approached the persona & behavior work

I led the persona and behavior work as a structured design proposal, anchored in two frameworks (Persona Design for Conversational Interfaces, and Behavior Design) and applied to Landbot’s specific context. The design supervisor reviewed and shaped the framework with me. The document is what engineering and product build against.

Three personality traits, picked deliberately

Competent. Copilot knows Landbot well. It doesn’t guess, hedge for the sake of hedging, or give vague answers. When it suggests something, it’s grounded in how the builder actually works.

Efficient. It respects the user’s time. Responses are as long as they need to be and no longer. Action over conversation when there’s a choice.

Transparent. It surfaces uncertainty rather than hiding it. It tells the user when something’s outside its scope. It doesn’t paper over doubt with confident-sounding language.

Warmth was deliberately not a fourth trait. At the frequency a power user will hit Copilot, performative friendliness turns into friction. Calm reliability ends up reading as warmer than enthusiasm theater anyway.

Tone, on four spectrums

Tone isn’t binary, so I positioned Copilot on four axes:

SpectrumPositionWhy
Formal ↔ CasualSlightly casualA workspace, not a boardroom. Contractions yes, slang no, emojis no.
Expert ↔ NoviceExpert-leaningUses Landbot vocabulary (blocks, flows, fields, channels) without over-explaining.
Warm ↔ ColdNeutrally warmAcknowledges intent without pleasantries. “Got it, here’s the flow,” not “Great question!”
Animated ↔ CalmSteadily calmNo exclamation marks. Steady energy. This matters when users hit Copilot hundreds of times.

Power dynamics that shift with context

Most copilot designs pick one mode and commit. Copilot’s relationship to the user moves based on what’s happening.

The three modes, and why the relationship isn't fixed

The default is peer collaborator: “Here’s what I’d suggest.” It shifts toward expert guide when the user looks new or stuck, and toward subordinate tool when the request is clear and specific. The signals driving the shift are things like account age, builder activity level, complexity of the request, and how full the canvas already is.

The reason this isn’t fixed: a power user getting the “let me explain everything” treatment feels patronized. A new user getting curt confirmations feels lost. One mode can’t serve both, and we needed to serve both.

A scenario map, not a script

Behavior was specified across seven scenarios, each with a defined response posture. The ones that mattered most:

The five scenarios that mattered most

First interaction. No tutorial monologue. A brief orientation grounded in the actual canvas state. If the canvas is empty, “What kind of bot do you want to build?” If the canvas has blocks, “I can see X blocks. Want me to review them or add to the flow?”

Asked to do something it can’t. Direct, no faking it. “I can’t do that right now. Here’s what I can help with instead.” If the request is close to something it can do, it bridges: “I can’t edit your WhatsApp template directly, but I can set up the flow that sends it.”

Mid-execution interruption. Stop immediately. Confirm where things stand: “Stopped. I had completed X but hadn’t started Y. Want me to undo, or keep it?” No arguing, no finishing “just one more thing.”

Uncertainty. Surface it explicitly through the streaming thinking block. Never guess silently and apply. Uncertainty pauses execution and asks.

A mistake. Acknowledge it right away and point to undo. “That wasn’t right. Let me undo that and try again.” The undo system is the safety net the behavior actively uses.

What Copilot won’t do

The limits matter as much as the capabilities. v2 explicitly cannot:

  • Take destructive actions (delete bots, remove published bots, reset analytics) without manual user action
  • Pretend to know things it doesn’t, or fake capability it lacks
  • Make API calls on the user’s behalf or test external endpoints
  • Read conversation logs or raw analytics data
  • Give business strategy, legal advice, or claims about conversion rates

These aren’t engineering constraints I copied down. They’re design choices. Each one is a place where the easy thing, letting Copilot try, letting it answer broadly, letting it act fast, would have traded short-term helpfulness for long-term trust. v2 picks trust every time.

🛠️ Solution

Four decisions that shaped Copilot v2

The design moves that determined how Copilot feels in use. Not every screen, just the ones that mattered.

1. A right-side panel, always visible, never in the way

Copilot lives in the right rail of the builder, persistent and out of the canvas.

The left rail is where users configure individual blocks, focused single-object work. If we’d put Copilot there, it would have been competing with that mode every time someone clicked into a block. Putting it on the right keeps it in peripheral vision while users work, available without interrupting block-level configuration.

Considered

Copilot on the left, sharing space with block configurations.

Why we didn’t

It would have collapsed two distinct modes into the same physical space, and forced users to choose between them every time they clicked.

2. Apply / Discard preview cards instead of direct execution

Copilot proposes changes as previewable cards. The user applies or discards. Nothing touches the canvas without approval.

The fastest version of Copilot would have let it act directly, with undo as the safety net. We didn’t ship that. A user’s flow is their work, and watching Copilot rearrange it without permission, even reversibly, is the wrong default for a tool whose job is to build trust.

The card lays out every change before it lands: the blocks Copilot wants to add, the order they’ll connect in, the full path from start to finish. The user reads it, then chooses. It’s slower in the happy case. It’s a lot safer in the unhappy one.

Considered

Direct application with undo as the only safety net.

Why we didn’t

It puts the burden of correction on the user, and it treats the canvas as Copilot’s space to edit rather than the user’s space to own.

3. Visible thought process instead of hidden reasoning

Copilot streams its reasoning in a collapsible block above each response. Users can see what it’s doing and why, in real time.

Most chat interfaces hide reasoning behind a clean answer. We made the opposite call. Users won’t trust an AI that delivers confident answers without showing its work, especially when those answers are about to modify their bot.

The streaming thought process does three things at once. It explains the why behind every action. It surfaces uncertainty before it becomes a mistake. And it teaches users how Copilot reads their canvas. Power users collapse it. New users learn from it. It’s there if they need it.

Considered

Hidden reasoning with a clean response only, like most consumer chat tools.

Why we didn’t

In a builder context, transparency is the trust mechanism. A wrong action delivered with confident language erodes the product faster than a slower visible one.

4. Per-bot conversation history

Every bot keeps its own Copilot conversation history. Switching bots switches context. Returning to a bot picks up where the user left off.

Copilot’s value compounds across a session. By the third or fourth interaction, it’s seen the canvas, learned the user’s pattern, and can respond in shorthand. Wiping that context every time the user switches bots, or comes back the next day, would reset the relationship to zero each time.

Per-bot history makes Copilot persistent in the right place: tied to the work, not the user. Within a single bot, users can spin up multiple conversations: one for setting up an integration, another for fixing a broken flow, a third for adding new logic. Each thread stays focused on its own task instead of becoming a single sprawling log.

Considered

A single continuous thread per bot, where everything accumulates over time.

Why we didn’t

Users work on distinct tasks within the same bot, integrating Calendly, then fixing a broken flow, then adding lead qualification. One mega-thread mixes them. Separate threads keep each task scannable and let users return to a specific conversation without scrolling through unrelated history.

⚖️ Trade-offs

What v2 deliberately didn’t ship

v2 covers the core trust loop: propose, preview, apply, undo. But not everything in the persona and behavior proposal made it into the first cut. Two cuts mattered more than the others.

Build/Chat mode split

The persona doc proposed two distinct postures. Build mode, where Copilot executes with previews and confirmations. Chat mode, where Copilot explains and plans without touching the canvas. The split was clean on paper.

What we cut was the user-facing toggle, the version where the user picks the mode. The version we actually want is smarter: Copilot infers from the user’s input whether they’re asking to build or to understand, and adopts the right posture without making them say so.

Shipping the toggle would have been faster. We didn’t, because asking the user to declare their mode every time is exactly the kind of friction we built v2 to remove. The right design is contextual, and that’s the work that’s in flight now.

Status: in design for v2.1.

Adaptive tone

The persona doc proposed that Copilot’s tone shift with context. Warmer for first interactions and onboarding. Calmer and more efficient for returning power users. More careful when about to make destructive changes.

We shipped a single tone (calm, neutrally warm, expert-leaning). Not because the adaptive version was wrong, but because we needed to know that the single tone worked across user types before adding variability on top. Shipping adaptive tone before the base tone was right would have meant debugging two layers at the same time when something felt off.

Status: deferred until base tone is validated in production.

📊 Results

Five months in, the design thesis is holding up

v2 went in front of users in late January 2026. The metrics aren’t a clean v0-vs-v2 comparison, the rebuild changed too many things at once for that to be honest, but they show whether the design is doing its job: helping users get from intent to a working bot.

Headline: Copilot to publish, climbing month over month

Among trial users who interact with Copilot to make an action on the canvas, the share who go on to publish a bot has risen from 46% in December 2025 to 64% in April 2026. That’s an 18-point lift over five months, on a base of more than 5,000 Copilot interactions.

The trend tracks v2’s rollout and iteration. Early months reflect rough edges. Later months reflect the design and behavior refinements as they landed. To be honest, this isn’t a pure design win. Prompt engineering and platform improvements pulled their weight too. But the design is the loop that makes any of it visible to the user: previews, undo, transparent reasoning. Without those, faster prompts wouldn’t translate into more publishes.

Activation: Copilot users move further than manual builders

Among users who finish onboarding, those who use Copilot activate (start three or more conversations with their bot) at roughly 16%. Manual builders sit at 12%. Users who do neither sit at 3%.

The gap between Copilot and manual is real but moderate. The bigger gap, the one between building anything and building nothing, is where most of the drop-off lives. That confirms what the original problem was always about: the hardest step is getting users from blank canvas to first build. Copilot pulls users across that step better than the canvas alone.

Repeat usage: about one in five come back the same day

Of users who start a Copilot conversation, 15-19% start a second one within 24 hours, with peaks above 40% in heavier-engagement weeks.

This isn’t classic retention, it’s a same-day return signal. What it tells us is that Copilot is sticky enough to be useful more than once in a session. Which matters, because the persona work was built around the assumption that power users would hit Copilot hundreds of times. The early data suggests they’re hitting it often enough for that bet to be worth defending.

The honest limits of this data

None of this is a controlled experiment. The publish trend is encouraging but confounded with engineering improvements. The activation gap is real but moderate. Stickiness is promising but needs more time to read. The signals are good enough to keep building on, and clear enough to keep questioning.

💭 Reflection

Two things I’d carry into the next AI product

Persona and behavior work belongs upstream of engineering scope, not in parallel. I led the persona and behavior work while the v2 rebuild was already being scoped technically. Some of the decisions I wanted to make, the Build/Chat mode split, adaptive tone, ran into architectural walls that wouldn’t have been there if the framework had come first. Next time, I’d push for persona and behavior to be the first deliverable of a redesign, not the one that catches up to engineering.

Calm and competent beats warm and animated for high-frequency AI. The team’s instinct going in (and the broader industry default) was that an AI assistant should feel friendly. The persona work and the usage patterns both pushed back on that. At the frequency a power user hits Copilot, performative warmth turns into friction. The next AI product I work on, I’ll start from “what’s the smallest amount of personality this needs?” instead of “what kind of personality should this have?” The default question we were asking was the wrong one.

Niklas Puertolas · Product Designer in Barcelona
