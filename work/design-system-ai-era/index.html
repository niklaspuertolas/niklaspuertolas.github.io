📝 TL;DR

Landbot’s marketing site ran on a traditional flow: I designed landings in Figma, dev implemented, the team reviewed. It worked — but it was getting slow next to what AI made possible, so people across the company started vibecoding their own pages with Claude, Lovable, and Stitch. Fast, but off-brand. The design system existed; it just lived in Figma, and nobody opens Figma mid-prompt.

So I rebuilt it to live where they already worked: landbot-org.github.io/web-design-system, in GitHub, with a Claude Skill on people’s machines. Now anyone on marketing can ask Claude for a landing page and it comes out looking like Landbot.

🏆 A non-designer (our paid marketer) shipped 15 on-brand landing-page variants on his own — no design or dev review. Presented company-wide at Demo Day, Q1 2026.

I built it solo with Claude Code, while Copilot v2 shipped, alongside the AI Product Designer Pro program at The Hero Camp.

🎯 Context

I’m a Product Designer at Landbot. My role splits across product and the marketing site, which means I sit between two surfaces: the in-app builder (Copilot v2, onboarding, UX systems) and the public web (landing pages, brand, the marketing site itself).

The web side had a process that had worked fine for years. Marketing brief → I designed in Figma → got the OK → dev implemented → team reviewed. Repeat for the next campaign.

It wasn’t anyone’s fault that this was slow. It was structural. Each landing needed at least three people in sequence, and each handoff added time. A typical landing took from a few days to a few weeks depending on complexity.

I’d been watching AI tools change what “fast” meant for a while. Claude, Lovable, Stitch. I was using them myself, both at work and in side projects, and I could see where this was going. The team’s reference point for how long a landing should take was shifting from weeks to hours, and the old process was starting to feel slow.

🧩 The problem

Marketers started building without us. The design system couldn’t keep up.

Once AI tools were good enough to generate landings, colleagues and leadership stopped waiting. They were vibecoding pages on their own with Claude. Fast. But the output didn’t look like Landbot. Different gradients, different button radius, off-brand typography. Each page felt like it came from a different company.

The instinct was to push back: use the design system, follow the brand. But the design system at that point was a Figma file. To “use it” meant opening Figma, finding the right component, copying values into your code by hand. Nobody was going to do that mid-prompt, and honestly, I couldn’t tell them to. They were getting work done faster than the old flow ever did. The system had to adapt to them, not the other way around.

Four things were happening at once:

  • Landings took days to weeks in the traditional flow
  • Marketing and leadership were vibecoding to get around it
  • What they shipped wasn’t on-brand
  • The speed was real. The shortcut wasn’t going away

That last point was the one that mattered. The design system was the thing that needed to change.

🔍 Research and framing

What I learned from doing it myself

The short version: I’d been vibecoding myself, so I knew the friction firsthand — Claude writes clean HTML/CSS but has no idea what brand it’s in. The fix wasn’t better prompts; it was giving it the right context, structured so it could use it. Rebuilding the tokens in code even made the system more rigorous than it had been in Figma.

What doing it myself taught me

I’d already been vibecoding myself. The friction was familiar: Claude could write structurally sound HTML and CSS, but with no idea what brand it was supposed to be working in. Default Tailwind colors, generic shadows, Inter instead of DM Sans. The fix wasn’t prompt engineering. It was giving the AI the right context, structured in a way it could actually use.

When I started rebuilding the tokens in code, something useful happened. Token relationships that lived as loose variables in Figma had to become a real architecture in JSON. The translation forced a level of rigor that Figma had let me skip. The system got better in the move.

I was also in the middle of the AI Product Designer Pro program at The Hero Camp. The program was teaching how to design systems where AI is a primary consumer. The timing helped: I was learning the theory and applying it to a real problem in the same week.

Four insights that framed the system

1. The real consumer of a design system had changed. Not just the designers and engineers it used to serve, but the marketers, content folks, and operators who were now generating work through AI. The system had to serve that pair.

2. AI assistants had become the actual reader of the system. If Claude couldn’t parse it, the human-facing documentation didn’t matter how pretty it looked. It had to be machine-readable first, human-browseable second.

3. GitHub was where AI could act. Claude via MCP can technically reach into Figma now, create frames, read content. But the workflow is slow, eats a lot of tokens, and isn’t always reliable. Code is direct. AI can clone a repo, read the tokens, generate against them, and the round trip costs almost nothing. The source of truth had to move to where the consumer could operate cleanly.

4. Brand consistency was a guardrails problem, not a training one. Nobody was going to sit through brand training sessions for a system they were about to use through an AI assistant. The path of least resistance had to be the on-brand path.

🧭 Defining the system’s audience and surface

Three audiences, one primary

The system serves three groups, but it’s optimized for one.

Primary: AI assistants. Claude, Cursor, and whatever comes next. The system has to be readable and unambiguous to a model. If this audience is served well, the others get served indirectly.

Secondary: The marketing team. Content, paid, growth, copy. People who aren’t designers and don’t write code by hand, but have access to AI assistants that can. They never touch the system directly. They talk to Claude, and the system runs in the background.

Tertiary: Engineers and designers. The people maintaining and evolving the system, and the occasional Webflow theme that still consumes the tokens directly.

Four surfaces

The system itself is a small set of pieces that work together:

  • Token architecture in four layers (primitives, semantic, maps, responsive)
  • Component library of HTML+CSS snippets
  • SKILL.md that tells AI when and how to apply everything
  • Public documentation site at landbot-org.github.io/web-design-system for the humans who want to browse it

The same content serves both machines and humans. The tokens are JSON for AI, and CSS variables on the doc site for humans. The components are HTML+CSS snippets that either can copy. The duplication is intentional.

🛠️ Solution

Four decisions that shaped the system

These were the moves that determined what the system actually does. Not every detail, just the ones that mattered.

1. GitHub as source of truth, not Figma

The most contrarian decision. GitHub is canonical. Figma still exists, but only for early exploration and wireframing.

In most design systems, Figma is canonical and code is downstream. We flipped it. GitHub is canonical. Figma still exists, but only for early exploration and wireframing. The moment a decision matters, it lives in the repo.

Why: because AI can read and write GitHub cleanly. Claude can clone the repo, parse the tokens, reference the components, and generate code that uses them correctly. The same isn’t true of Figma, at least not yet. MCP makes Figma reachable in theory, but in practice it’s slow, expensive in tokens, and unreliable enough that you can’t build a workflow on it. Code is direct. If your primary consumer is AI, you put the source of truth where AI can reach it without friction.

The side effect: translating the system into code forced architectural decisions that Figma had let me skip. Token relationships that were implicit became explicit. The system got more rigorous in the move.

Considered

Keeping Figma as source of truth and syncing tokens to code via something like Tokens Studio.

Why we didn’t

Sync is fragile. Every export is a place where things drift. And even with perfect sync, AI still can’t read Figma cleanly. The bottleneck wasn’t sync, it was reach.

2. Token architecture in four layers

The tokens aren’t one flat file. They’re organized in four layers, each with a clear job.

The four layers:

  • 01-primitives.json — Raw values. Every hex, every px, every duration. The atoms.
  • 02-semantic.json — Named aliases. brand.primary points to a primitive. Where meaning gets attached to values.
  • 03-maps.json — Component-level decisions. Button sizes, card specs.
  • 04-responsive.json — Mobile vs desktop overrides.

This separation is how AI reasons about the system. When Claude is asked to make a button, it pulls from maps (which says “buttons use —radius-4”). When asked to add a new color, it knows to add to primitives first and then alias in semantic. The layers act as guardrails for how decisions cascade.

Considered

A flat token file, simpler to write, easier to scan.

Why we didn’t

Flat tokens collapse the difference between “this is the hex value” and “this is the brand color.” Without that distinction, every refactor breaks consumers. Layered tokens are more work upfront, but they’re the contract that makes the system safe to evolve.

3. A Claude Skill that points to the repo

The piece that closes the loop. Without it, the system exists but nobody knows how to invoke it.

With it, the system runs automatically every time someone on the team asks Claude to build or review web UI for Landbot.

The skill is a SKILL.md file with a frontmatter description that tells Claude exactly when to activate: “creating, editing, or reviewing any HTML, CSS, or web component intended for Landbot’s marketing website.” When someone asks Claude “build me a hero section for the new pricing page,” Claude reads that description, recognizes the trigger, and pulls in the tokens and components automatically.

It installs once per machine via a symlink to the user’s Claude skills folder. After that, it’s invisible. The person just talks to Claude normally and the brand context is always there.

Considered

A long custom prompt that marketers would paste at the start of every conversation.

Why we didn’t

That’s exactly the friction we built the system to remove. If using the design system requires the user to remember to invoke it, most people won’t.

4. Documentation for humans and machines, from the same source

The system needed to be browseable by humans (someone wants to see what a button looks like) and parseable by AI (Claude needs the exact CSS). I built one source that serves both.

The doc site at landbot-org.github.io/web-design-system is the human view: rendered components, sidebar navigation, all the visual references a designer or PM would want. The same components are also extracted as individual HTML+CSS files in docs/components/, which is what AI pulls from. The CSS variables in the doc site’s :root block are the canonical token values.

AI reads the JSON, humans read the rendered page, but the underlying truth is shared.

🔒 Governance

The repo is read-only for the team

Only I, as maintainer, push changes. If someone needs a new component or a new token, they message me with a description and screenshots, I review, and I push.

This sounds bottlenecked, but it isn’t. The system itself isn’t where the volume is. The volume is in landing pages, which the team now builds independently with AI. The system only changes when there’s a genuine new need, and those are rare.

The README puts it directly:

Tokens are stable contracts. Adding new ones is fine. Renaming or removing them breaks downstream consumers (every Landbot teammate’s Claude skill, every Webflow theme, every PR Claude has ever generated). The maintainer reviews these changes carefully.

That last clause, every PR Claude has ever generated, is what tells you the system is being used at real scale.

⚖️ Trade-offs

Figma didn’t die. It got reassigned.

I didn’t kill Figma. I moved it. It’s still the right tool for early exploration and visual thinking before a decision is made. It’s where a new section layout starts. But the moment that layout becomes a decision the team relies on, it moves to the repo. Figma stops being authoritative the second the system needs to act on it.

This took some adjustment. The instinct as a designer is to keep Figma as home. Letting it be only the exploration layer felt like demoting a tool I’d spent years working in. In practice, it freed Figma to be what it’s actually best at, a fast sketch space, and freed the repo to be what AI needs, a parseable contract.

The output still needs iteration sometimes

I want to be honest about where this is. AI doesn’t always produce perfect output. Sometimes a marketer generates a landing and the structure is right but a section needs work. Sometimes Claude misses a brand detail. Sometimes someone gets stuck and needs help finishing.

When that happens, I come in and help with the final pass. That’s still the right trade. We’re at the beginning of this, and a system that takes a marketer 90% of the way and then needs a designer for the last 10% is dramatically better than a system that needs a designer for 100%. Over time, both the tools and the system get better, and that 10% shrinks.

The goal isn’t a system that replaces design judgment. It’s one that puts design judgment in the right place: at the edges where it actually matters, not at every step of producing a standard component.

What’s not in v1

The system covers the marketing site only, not the in-product UI. The product team is doing a similar shift with their own design system in parallel, and the future goal is to unify what can be unified. But trying to do both at once would have meant doing neither well. Marketing was the urgent surface, because that’s where the vibecoding was happening, so that’s where the system shipped first.

📊 Results

Demo Day, real adoption, and 15 landings on the same page

Three things to report.

Demo Day. I presented the system to the whole company at our internal Demo Day in Q1 2026. This was the formal introduction: how the system worked, how to install the skill, and what it meant for the team. The presentation moved the project from “side experiment” to “the way we build web work at Landbot.”

The paid marketer’s 15 variants. The clearest single piece of evidence that the system works in the hands of someone non-technical. Our paid marketer needed to A/B test a landing page concept and built 15 variants on his own, using Claude with the skill installed. All 15 were on-brand. None of them passed through me or through dev before going out for testing. That’s the loop the system was built to enable: someone with a marketing question generates the design artifact themselves, and the brand stays intact automatically.

Day-to-day shifts. Three things changed at once in the team’s workflow:

  • Marketing got faster. Iteration loops on landings dropped from days or weeks to hours.
  • I got my time back. The hours I used to spend on Figma landings now go to product work.
  • Brand consistency improved. Not because people are trying harder, but because the path of least resistance is now the on-brand path.
The honest limits of this

This isn’t a controlled experiment. I can’t show a clean before/after on speed because the team and the tooling both changed at the same time. The signals are consistent though: real adoption, real outputs, real time saved.

💭 Reflection

Two things I’d carry into the next system

The user of a design system has changed. Most design systems haven’t caught up. I didn’t build a better design system; I built one whose primary user is a machine, with humans interacting indirectly through it. Once I accepted that reframe, every decision (GitHub over Figma, layered tokens, the skill, the read-only governance) followed from it. Without it, I’d have built the same kind of system as everyone else and gotten the same kind of adoption problem.

AI didn’t replace the designer, it changed where the design work happens. I used to spend my web time making landing pages. Now I spend it making the system that makes the landing pages, and stepping in for the last-mile work the system can’t quite handle yet. Less visible, more leveraged. The product team is heading toward a similar shift with their own design system. The future is two systems that eventually become one. We’re early in this. The interesting work is figuring out what it looks like when we’re not.

Niklas Puertolas · Product Designer in Barcelona
